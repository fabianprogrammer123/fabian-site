thoughts

on China — june 2026

After ten days in China, a few observations.

The wealth gap is doing a lot of work. The delta between rich and poor is enormous, which lets China employ a vast number of cheap service workers and laborers. The result is a very high level of comfort in the cities, excellent conditions for doing business, and highly attractive tourism. More importantly, it enables an unbelievably fast infrastructure buildout — crews working construction sites 24/7. There are literally more empty apartments in China than there are people in Germany. It nearly triggered a financial crisis, and things still aren't fully stable, but it shows how far the buildout has run. Decades of European outsourcing seem to have produced ever better-developed networks of factories and supply chains, and China's wealth and power are built on that foundation.

Recipe for success. No worker unions and fast replacement. Strategic development zones: build the basic infrastructure, then sell off to developers, with regulation that enables — and enforces — deadlines. Basic infrastructure first: workers, energy, market access. Competition engineered between districts and between companies. Science documentaries playing on the trains. And the headline result: 1.4 billion people lifted out of poverty.

What surprised me. Low English skills. A high appreciation for European manufacturing and luxury brands. An aging population — how is that the outcome of deliberate social engineering? Xiaomi significantly beating Apple by building an EV as a consumer-electronics company with far less market cap, simply through manufacturing supply chains, ecosystem, and expertise. Unitree in the process of beating Boston Dynamics badly. Shenzhen with an entrepreneurial buzz comparable to SF, and better access to robotics components and electronics manufacturing than anywhere else.

Challenges. Youth unemployment. Surveillance that limits perceived freedom and pushes rebellion underground — I don't think that's a sustainable state of existence, though this may be my libertarian bias. Power conflicts between the party and companies (Jack Ma, real-estate executives). And access to global capital markets, where Taiwan is the perfect point of leverage.

So what? The thing I keep returning to is that China's edge is less about any single technology than about a system tuned end-to-end for execution speed — capital, labor, infrastructure, and competition all pointed at shipping physical things fast. The open question for me is how durable that is once the cheap-labor delta narrows and the political-economic frictions compound.

on Limitless Mind — april 2026

Some notes to myself on the mind, and on how much of what we call reality is actually interpretation.

1. Almost everything we perceive is, in part, a mental concept and an interpretation — and therefore partly ours. Because we share so many perceptions, we mistake them for "objective truth". Trust your own perception and stand by it, but stay genuinely, and playfully, curious about everyone else's.

2. Fantasy quickly confronts you with aloneness when others don't share it. The childlike lightness is worth keeping anyway — with enough intensity, consistency, and the courage to stand by your own impulses.

3. There is a deep calm in remembering that the world and the universe continue with or without me. Anxiety tends to dissolve once you understand yourself as a fixed part of the world, rather than something bound to physical existence in the future. At both the largest and the smallest scale, fear stops making much sense.

4. Change is not a threat. It is a neutral transformation, and simply another part of this world.

on AI adoption — march 2026

Since starting my research at Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI), I realized 3 points are not publicly discussed with sufficient clarity:

1. The delta of AI Adoption (whats technically possible vs. whats practically implemented) is more significant than you might think. And this delta is relevant for almost all jobs!

2. There are massive individual differences in productivity uplift. OpenAI calls this "Capability Overhang". They measured across 70+ countries and found that power users leverage 7x more thinking capabilities than average ones. This underscores that using AI is clearly, to some extent, a learnable skill — probably a bit like a mixture of managing a team, formulating a research question and critically reviewing an essay.

3. It's a huge problem that compute, data, AI talent, and funding are already so centralized in the hands of a few people, companies, and countries. Most people on earth have never even used an LLM! Given the 2 points above, it is very important that more people experiment with LLMs, take bolder measures to catch up in adoption, and ensure that productivity gains translate into actual improvements of our society!

on altruism — december 2025

I signed the Giving What We Can pledge — 10% of all future earnings. Not because I feel perfectly aligned with the EA community, but because it's the best approximation for moral accountability I see right now. A pledge externalizes a commitment, so that my future, wealthier, more rationalizing self can't quietly negotiate it away.

A few things I keep coming back to:

1. Giving is easier to defend as a rule than as a case-by-case decision. No single euro makes me generous — but a consistent rule, compounded over a life, does.

2. I'd rather be much than have much. "Es ist besser viel zu sein, als viel zu haben." (Karl Marx)

3. Effectiveness matters as much as intention. Founding Lupila Learns taught me that good intentions and good outcomes are very different things — and only the second one actually helps anyone.

on Africa — october 2024

Africa is where I learned the distance between talking about a problem and actually solving one. With Lupila Learns we support schools in Tanzania with educational infrastructure, and the lesson that stuck is how far "helping" is from "helping effectively". Logistics, maintenance, local ownership, and incentives matter far more than the size of the initial gesture.

I'm increasingly convinced the continent is one of the most interesting places to build this decade — young, mobile-first, and underserved by exactly the kind of infrastructure that compounds. Companies like emata (agricultural lending in Uganda) are the shape of what I want to pay more attention to.

foundational beliefs — august 2024

"Change is not only always possible, but a requirement of history."

1. Everything is in constant transition, which is a contrast to our state-specific way of perceiving the world.

2. While it is more likely that there is no meaning in anything, we have no other option than acting as if there was one.

3. The best approximation to meaning for me seems to develop an actionable world model upon which one can make accurate predictions about fundamental relationships, improve my own openness and ability to act upon my beliefs, and with that to pursue happiness for the largest amount of people possible.
